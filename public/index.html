UNIT 3

I always knew failure was an important part of learning but after reading part of Amy Edmondson’s Right Kind of Wrong, I understood that just failing isn’t enough if we do not understand the type of failure and make it legible to break down and learn from.

The book spoke of three distinct failure types: basic, complex, and intelligent. This was an unfamiliar concept to me. I always looked at failure as a monolithic thing, as Edmondson put it best; something that happens when you aren’t careful enough or not prepared enough. The concept of intelligent failures interested me the most. In the book, Amy talks about an example from her journey into the scientific community where what she initially perceived as a disaster when her research data suggested that better hospital teams made more mistakes instead of the other way around, as intuition might suggest. This “wrong” result, as the prologue explains, was her first intelligent failure. The “failure” didn’t end her career but revealed that high-performing teams felt safe enough to report errors, which led to her discovering psychological safety. This example changed my perspective on how to look at results that don’t justify the thesis of whatever I am working on.
